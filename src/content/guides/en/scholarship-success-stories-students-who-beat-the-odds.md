## Short answer

**Fully funded scholarships are not only for students from rich families or famous schools.** Each year, students who grew up in slums, war zones and refugee camps win places at universities like Oxford, Princeton and Wesleyan, with their fees and living costs paid. Below are five true stories, rewritten from public sources, and the lessons they share. If you want to start your own story, browse the [funded opportunities open now](/opportunities/).

## 1. Kennedy Odede (Kenya): from the streets of Kibera to Wesleyan University

Kennedy Odede's family left their village in Kenya after a famine, when he was two years old. They ended up in **Kibera, one of Africa's largest informal settlements**, where homes had no sewage system and basic health care and schooling were hard to reach. His mother could not afford the **10 US dollars a year** needed for primary school fees, and at ten years old he was living on the streets.

As a young man he worked in a factory for about **10 US cents an hour**. With that small income, in 2004, he started a community group in Kibera called **Shining Hope for Communities (SHOFCO)**. His work and drive caught attention, and in **2008 he arrived at Wesleyan University in the United States on a full scholarship**, even though he had very little formal schooling.

He graduated in **2012 with honours in sociology** and spoke at his graduation ceremony. He went back to Kenya to lead SHOFCO, which grew into one of the largest grassroots organisations in Kibera.

**Lesson:** what you have already done for your community can matter as much as your certificates.

## 2. Summia Tora (Afghanistan): the first Afghan Rhodes Scholar

Summia Tora fled Afghanistan as a child with her family, escaping violence and economic hardship, and grew up as a **refugee in Pakistan**, where she did most of her schooling. She found the United World Colleges (UWC) through an online search and won a **scholarship to UWC-USA in New Mexico** for her last two years of high school.

Her selection test took place in **Kabul**. The day after her test, an attack on a hotel in the city killed nine people, including **Dr Roshan Thomas, the head of the UWC selection committee**, who had encouraged young people to come back and work for change in Afghanistan. Summia has said this was the main reason she applied.

She went on to **Earlham College** in the United States, where she studied Economics and Peace and Global Studies, interned with a refugee resettlement agency, and started projects that won funding, including menstrual health workshops for girls in Afghanistan and Pakistan. In **2019 she was named a Rhodes Scholar**, the first from Afghanistan. The Rhodes Scholarship pays Oxford course fees, a yearly stipend, the visa fee and flights. She planned to study refugee and forced migration issues at Oxford.

**Lesson:** use the internet to search for programmes, and turn your own experience into a clear goal.

## 3. Asad Hussein (Somali refugee, Kenya): from Dadaab to Princeton and Oxford

Asad Hussein was **born in 1996 in Dadaab**, in Kenya, one of the largest refugee camps in the world. His parents and older sister had fled unrest in Somalia a few years before. In the camp, refugees could not legally work, and he needed a permit just to leave.

He finished high school in **2014** and then spent about **three years** trying to get into a university. He did not give up, and **Princeton University finally accepted him with a full scholarship**, worth about 70,000 US dollars for his first year alone. He studied literature at Princeton, and later became a **Mastercard Foundation Scholar** at the University of Oxford, studying for an MSc in Refugee and Forced Migration Studies. His writing has appeared in The New York Times, The Guardian and Foreign Policy.

**Lesson:** a gap of several years is not the end. Keep building your skills and keep applying.

## 4. Hawo Jehow Siyad (Somalia): top of her county, then a DAFI scholarship

Hawo Jehow Siyad arrived in Kenya in **2000, at six years old, with her grandmother**. She grew up in **Hagadera, one of the camps in Dadaab**, and completed both primary and secondary school there.

In **2012 she ranked first among all schools in Garissa County**. The next year she won a **DAFI scholarship**, a programme run by UNHCR and funded by the German government and other donors. DAFI covers most university tuition fees plus allowances for housing, food, books and local transport. She studied **Economics and Statistics at the University of Nairobi** and graduated.

After graduating she chose to return to Somalia. She first worked at the Ministry of Transport and Aviation and then joined a World Bank funded government project as a database officer.

**Lesson:** strong exam results open doors, even in very hard conditions. Refugee students should look for programmes made for them, such as DAFI.

## 5. Farouq Aldandashi (Syria): from war-torn Homs to a Chevening Scholarship

At 17, while living in **Homs during the war in Syria**, Farouq Aldandashi began volunteering with the Red Cross. He stayed for almost **seven years** while also studying for his degree. After he graduated, wartime inflation made his income too small to live on, so he left his family and moved to **Turkey**, where he worked for three years with the International Medical Corps and CARE.

As a Syrian, he found that getting a visa was very difficult. Still, he won a **Chevening Scholarship**, the UK government's international award, and studied an **MSc in Disaster Management and Sustainable Development at Northumbria University** in Newcastle. Chevening pays tuition fees, a monthly living stipend, return flights and the visa fee.

**Lesson:** years of volunteer and humanitarian work are real experience. Scholarship committees value them.

## What do these stories have in common?

- **They served their communities first.** Every student had a record of helping others before they applied.
- **They used free information.** Online searches, school libraries and organisations like UNHCR led them to programmes they had never heard of.
- **They did not stop after setbacks.** Long gaps, displacement and closed doors did not end their plans.
- **They applied to programmes built for students like them.** Rhodes, Chevening, Mastercard Foundation and DAFI all aim to fund talent that cannot pay.
- **They had a clear purpose.** Each one could explain how their studies would help people back home. Learn to show this in your own words with our guide on [how to write a motivation letter](/guides/how-to-write-a-motivation-letter/).

## Fully funded scholarships exist, and you can apply too

You do not need money to study abroad if you win the right scholarship. Fully funded programmes usually cover tuition, living costs and often flights and visa fees. Some are open now on ScholarHelp, for example the **[Gates Cambridge Scholarship](/opportunities/gates-cambridge-scholarship-2027/)**, the **[Clarendon Scholarships at Oxford](/opportunities/clarendon-scholarships-oxford-2027/)**, the **[Eiffel Excellence Scholarship](/opportunities/eiffel-excellence-scholarship-2027/)** and the **[Hansen Leadership Institute](/opportunities/hansen-leadership-institute-2027-usa/)**.
