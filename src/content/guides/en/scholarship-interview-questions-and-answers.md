## Short answer

**Most scholarship interviews ask the same few questions: who you are, why this programme, what you have done, what you will do after, and how you handle problems.** Prepare a short, true story for each one, practise saying it out loud in two minutes or less, and link every answer back to the programme's goals. Being clear and honest matters more than perfect English.

## What do interview panels want to find out?

The panel already has your documents. In the interview, they check three things:

- **That you are the person on paper.** They ask you to explain your projects, grades and plans in your own words.
- **That you fit the programme.** They want to see that you know what it offers and why it matters to you.
- **That you will use the chance well.** They look for clear plans, motivation and the ability to adapt in a new country or group.

## What are the most common questions, and how do I answer them?

**1. "Tell us about yourself."**
Give three short parts: what you study or do now, one achievement you are proud of, and what you want to do next. Keep it under one minute. Do not repeat your whole CV.

**2. "Why did you choose this programme?"**
Name two or three real things: a course, a lab, a supervisor, a city, or a skill the programme teaches. Then say how each one helps your plan. Read the programme's website again the day before.

**3. "Why should we choose you?"**
Point to one or two results with numbers if possible, such as a project you led, a group you trained or research you finished. Then link them to what the programme is looking for.

**4. "Tell us about a challenge you faced."**
Use a simple order: the situation, what you did, the result, and what you learned. Choose a real problem you solved, not a small one or one that makes someone else look bad.

**5. "What will you do after the programme?"**
Be specific and realistic. "I will go back to work at the health ministry and train ten colleagues in data analysis" is stronger than "I want to change my country". Many funded programmes, especially government scholarships, care about how you will use what you learn at home.

**6. "Do you have any questions for us?"**
Always have one ready. Ask about something you could not find online, such as how students usually choose their research topic or how alumni stay involved.

## How do I prepare in one week?

1. **Days 1 to 2:** reread your application, motivation letter and the programme page. Write down the three points you most want the panel to remember.
2. **Days 3 to 4:** write short notes (not a script) for the six questions above.
3. **Days 5 to 6:** practise out loud with a friend or teacher, or record yourself on your phone. Cut answers that go over two minutes.
4. **Day 7:** test your camera, microphone, internet and light. Prepare a quiet room, a glass of water and a copy of your application.

## What mistakes should I avoid?

- Memorising long answers word for word. It sounds stiff, and you may lose your place.
- Speaking badly of your country, university or past teachers.
- Saying things that do not match your written application.
- Asking only about money in your one question.
- Joining an online interview late. Log in five minutes early.

## What happens after the interview?

Send a short thank-you email if you have the panel's address, then wait for the result date given in your invitation. If you are not selected, ask politely for feedback; some programmes give it. Keep applying: there are always new [open opportunities](/opportunities/) on ScholarHelp.
