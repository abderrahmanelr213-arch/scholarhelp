## Short answer

**A strong summer research statement shows three things: what question excites you, what you have already done to explore it, and why this programme is the right next step.** Keep it within the word limit, use simple language, name the labs you want to join, and end with how the summer fits your plans. Specific examples beat big words every time.

## What do reviewers look for?

Reviewers in funded programmes, such as the ones in our [paid summer research internships guide](/guides/paid-summer-research-internships-abroad/), read hundreds of statements. They look for:

- **curiosity:** a real question you want to explore, not just "I love science"
- **evidence:** a class project, lab course, coding work or thesis where you did something yourself
- **fit:** a clear reason for this programme, this place and these labs
- **readiness:** signs that you can work independently, learn fast and finish what you start
- **purpose:** how the summer connects to your studies or career plans

## What is a simple structure that works?

1. **Opening (2–3 sentences).** Start with a moment or a question that made you curious. Avoid quotes and childhood stories.
2. **What you have done (1–2 paragraphs).** Describe one or two experiences in detail: the problem, what you did, what you learned. Numbers and methods help.
3. **Why this programme (1 paragraph).** Name the labs or topics you want to join and what interests you in their work. Mention anything unique about the programme.
4. **What you will bring (a few sentences).** Skills like programming, lab techniques, teamwork or languages.
5. **What comes next (2–3 sentences).** How the summer helps your next step: a thesis, a master's, a PhD or a career in research.

## Which mistakes should you avoid?

- going over the word limit or ignoring the exact question asked
- a list of grades and awards that already appear in your CV
- big claims with no examples ("I am very passionate and hardworking")
- naming the wrong university or lab after copying from another application
- writing only about yourself and never about the programme
- spelling mistakes. Read it aloud and ask a friend or teacher to check it

## How should you use your experience if you have little research?

Use what you have. A lab report, a group project, a hackathon, a data analysis for a class, or a problem you solved at work can all show research thinking. Explain the question, your method and the result, even if the result was a failure. Saying what you learned from a failed experiment often impresses reviewers more than a perfect story.

## What about the English requirement?

Many summer research programmes accept a letter showing your degree is taught in English, and some ask for no test at all. Read the language section of each official page, and see our guide on the [English proficiency (MOI) letter](/guides/english-proficiency-letter-instead-of-ielts/).
